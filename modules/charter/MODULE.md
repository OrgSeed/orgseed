# Module: charter

**For.** Turning the founder's goal into a charter the members can work from: the goal and its reason, a few near-term goals, what success looks like, and the roles the founder's own view of organizing suggests.

**Adds.** `org/CHARTER.md` (created at founding from the template below). Uses `org/GOAL.md` from the core.

**Works with.** `engagement` (progress is reported against the near-term goals), `decision-rights` (the charter names who signs).

**Costs.** Part of the founding session, and a short review whenever a near-term goal is met.

## Rules

### CH-1 The goal stays in the founder's words
rule: org/GOAL.md holds the goal, and why it matters, exactly as the founder put them. Members may propose a sharper wording, but only the founder's adopted wording stands.
why: a goal paraphrased by a member slowly becomes the member's goal, and the founder's own words are the check against that drift.
lit: Locke & Latham 2002, on goal commitment and the value of giving the goal's rationale [lead]

### CH-2 Break the goal into near-term goals
rule: Keep two to four near-term goals that can each be finished in a few sessions. When the question is new to everyone, make at least one of them a goal to learn something rather than a goal to produce something.
why: near-term goals let the founder see progress; when nobody yet knows how to do the work, a goal to learn something fits better than a goal to produce [opinion].
lit: Locke & Latham 2002, on proximal goals and learning goals for complex new tasks [lead]

### CH-3 Say what success looks like
rule: For each near-term goal, write one line on how the founder will know it is done.
why: without a stated test, "done" drifts toward whatever was produced.

### CH-4 Propose roles from the founder's own view of organizing
rule: When proposing roles or checks, start from how the founder says this work should be organized, and name the link ("You described ...; I propose ..."). Propose; never install.
why: the founder's knowledge of organizations is the design input; a structure they recognise is one they can judge and change.
lit: Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey 2018, on people using an algorithm they can modify [lead]

### CH-5 Show the charter back on one page
rule: After any change to the charter, show it back to the founder on one page and ask what to change. Record each change the founder makes as a decision.
why: a charter the founder has not seen is not their charter.

### CH-6 Name the side effects of goals
rule: If a goal starts to narrow the work in ways the founder did not intend (cutting corners, ignoring what the goal does not measure), say so plainly and propose a change.
why: a goal that focuses effort can also narrow it [opinion]; naming the narrowing lets the founder decide whether to change the goal.
lit: Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman 2009, on the side effects of goal setting [lead]

## Template: org/CHARTER.md

```
# Charter
Goal (founder's words): see org/GOAL.md
Near-term goals:
### NG-1 (title)
goal: ...
kind: produce | learn
done_when: ...
adopted_by: D-....
Who signs: the founder
Roles: see org/config.json
How this charter changes: by proposal and decision (AGENTS.md, CORE-2)
```

## Template: a project inside the organization (optional, draft)

For organizations whose work comes in bounded pieces. A project is proposed and decided like any structure. It is led by someone the founder names other than themself; it has people and AI members both, an end date, and, when it ends, a brief sourced account of what it did. Before work starts, four questions are answered in writing:
- What has each AI member been shaped by, and by which producer?
- At which points must a person look at the work before it goes on?
- What do the people know that the AI members do not, and the other way round?
- Who is accountable for each thing the project hands over?
When a project closes, its record goes in `work/`, and anything worth repeating becomes a proposal.

## Grounding

This module is here to turn the founder's goal into a charter the members can work from, because a goal paraphrased by a member slowly becomes the member's goal, and without a stated test "done" drifts toward whatever was produced. No theory is claimed for it, because none has been read for it. It is therefore a design guess, which a scholar may theorize and test, for example by running organizations with it on and with it off and comparing their records. Its `lit:` lines, where it has any, are leads unless they say read (`docs/LITERATURE.md`).
